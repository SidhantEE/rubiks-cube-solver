Research summary and bill of materials · 9 Nov 2026

What it takes to build a fast Rubik's cube solver

We are building Tier 1: six StepperOnline 17HE19-2004S stepper motors on TMC5160 drivers, about $440 to $470 in parts plus your own 24 V supply, aiming for a solve of 1 to 1.5 seconds. On paper the motor handles 40 to 45 ms quarter turns at 24 V.

The feasibility check is a calculation, not a measurement. No published cube robot names this exact motor, and cube friction is the main unknown. A one-axis bench test settles it before all six axes are built. The parts list below was audited on 10 Nov 2026.

Tier 1 motor choice

The pick is the StepperOnline 17HE19-2004S, chosen on price. At $6.36 it is the cheapest of the four, and it has lower winding inductance than the motor the Hackaday build used. Inductance is what starves a stepper of torque at speed. The LDO is the faster motor at five times the price.

Which motors have been documented in a cube solver

  • 17HS19-2004S1: yes. The Hackaday build's parts list links to this exact motor. It solved in 4.56 s, held back by weak TMC2208 drivers and a 75-move solver.
  • 17HE19-2004S (our pick): no cube solver names it. It is common in hobby CNC builds. Its wire colours differ from the 17HS19, and one builder had to swap the blue and green wires.
  • LDO-42STH48-2504AC: no cube solver names it. It is a standard high-speed 3D-printer motor.
  • Other fast stepper robots (0.900 s and 1.5 to 2 s) do not publish their motor models.

Will it work?

Yes on paper, for quarter turns of 40 ms and slower at 24 V

At 24 V the 17HE19-2004S has roughly 1.5 to 2.6 times the torque a 40 ms quarter turn needs. At 35 ms it has no margin on 24 V, but a 35 V supply restores it. About 20 moves at 40 to 45 ms is 0.8 to 0.9 s of motion.

My calculation. Assumes a cube layer of 150 g·cm² (a commenter's estimate on the MIT build log, close to my own), friction of 0.05 to 0.15 Nm (a guess), and a move that accelerates for half the turn and brakes for the other half. "Available" is the theoretical pull-out torque at peak speed; an open-loop stepper should stay well under it. The rotor inertia is 62 g·cm², from the motor's datasheet. The cube weighs 88.9 g, which puts one layer at roughly 145 g·cm², in line with the 150 used here. For comparison at 40 ms on 24 V, the same sum gives the LDO 0.48 Nm and the Hackaday motor 0.27 Nm.

Evidence from other builds

  • Same timing has been done with steppers. Flatland and Rose's 0.900 s record robot used six steppers at 50 full steps per quarter turn. Their published timing tables give 35 ms per quarter turn and 56 ms per half turn at the fastest setting, about 0.90 s for 25 moves. Their motor model is not published.
  • Generic steppers reach 1.5 to 2 s. iBoot32's 2022 robot did that with DRV8825 drivers, which are weaker than the TMC5160.
  • Too little torque fails outright. A University of Illinois team's 0.10 Nm NEMA 14 motors could not turn a face at all. Our motor has 5.5 times that at standstill.
  • No precedent for this exact motor. I read nine hobby cube robots and searched by part number. None names the 17HE19-2004S, and most name no motor.

What could still stop it

  • Cube friction. Nobody publishes a measured turning torque for a speed cube. A cube tensioned tight enough not to deform may need more than my 0.15 Nm upper guess.
  • Lock-ups. If one face stops a few degrees off, the next turn jams. An open-loop stepper then loses steps without knowing. The MIT team blew drive transistors this way.
  • Driver heat. The TMC5160 board needs a fan above 2 A. This motor runs at 2.0 A, right at that limit, and you have chosen to run without a fan. If a driver overheats it cuts the motor current, which shows up as missed turns in long runs.
  • Driver voltage ceiling. The standard TMC5160 board stops at 35 V, so a 36 V supply must be trimmed down to about 33 V.

What actually sets the solve time

Solve time is roughly the number of moves multiplied by the time per move, plus a few tens of milliseconds for the camera and solver. Both factors differ by about a factor of ten between the Hackaday build and the record.

  • Solver: switching from CFOP to a two-phase solver cuts 75 moves to about 20. It is free software and the largest single gain.
  • Parallel moves: rob-twophase counts opposite faces turning together as one move, averaging about 15 moves, found within 10 ms.
  • Torque over inertia: quarter-turn time depends on how hard the motor can accelerate its own rotor plus one cube layer. This is where steppers lose.
  • Corner cutting: starting the next face before the last one has stopped. It needs closed-loop position feedback and breaks cubes when mistimed.
  • The cube: at record speed, stock cubes snap. Purdue used a custom SLS nylon core tensioned too tight to turn by hand.

Checks against the parts' own documents

  • Motor: the 17HE19-2004S datasheet confirms 0.55 Nm, 2.0 A, 1.30 Ω, 2.40 mH, a 62 g·cm² rotor and 0.27 kg. Its drawing confirms a 5 mm shaft 24 mm long with a 15 mm flat, M3 holes on a 31 mm square and a 22 mm boss.
  • Motor cable: 1 m of 24 AWG wire ending in a 4-pin 2.54 mm plug. The wires run B−, A−, B+, A+, which does not match the driver's pin order, where each coil's two pins sit together. Two pins have to be moved in the plug before it goes on the driver.
  • Driver and motor: the driver's 3 A and 10 to 35 V limits cover the motor's 2.0 A on a 24 V supply.
  • Driver and Teensy: both use 3.3 V logic. The driver must be set up over SPI.
  • Driver header pins: I have not confirmed whether the board arrives with its pins soldered. Plan on a soldering iron being needed.
  • Cube: TheCubicle lists it at 56.0 mm and 88.9 g, magnetic.

What I would do

Build Tier 1, and bench-test one axis before buying six motors

The solver, camera reading, move scheduler and cube handling are the same work at every tier. Building them on steppers gets a working machine near 1 second, and a later brushless upgrade replaces only the actuators and their drivers.

  1. Solver on your computer. Build rob-twophase and get from a scramble to a move list. Free, and it works before any hardware arrives.
  2. One axis on a bench. One 17HE19-2004S motor and one TMC5160 on 24 V, turning one face of a tensioned speed cube. Start at 60 ms per quarter turn and shorten it until steps are lost. If it holds 40 to 45 ms, order the other five.
  3. Six axes with manual entry. Frame, six motors, a move scheduler. Type in the cube state and solve it.
  4. Parallel opposite faces. Run the two motors on an axis together, using the solver's axial mode.
  5. Cameras. Two cameras at opposite corners, fixed lighting, fixed pixel regions per sticker.
  6. Then upgrade actuators. Buy one brushless motor and driver, repeat the one-axis test, and only then buy the other five.

Open questions

  • Cube turning torque. Unmeasured. It is the largest uncertainty in the Tier 1 feasibility check.
  • How the motor grips the cube. Printed centre-cap adapters as in the Hackaday build, or metal square drives pressed into the centre recess as MIT did. Not yet decided.
  • Tools you have. A 3D printer covers Tier 1. Tier 2 wants metal square drives and a stiff frame.
  • Tier 2 motor inertia. I did not find a rotor inertia figure for the mj5208, so its quarter-turn time is untested.
  • Unread sources. Kollmorgen's site, The Verge and Design News blocked automated reading. The Verge article is the likeliest to hold more Purdue detail.
  • Prices. Most Tier 1 prices and several Tier 2 prices are my estimates. Reseller prices for Kollmorgen parts are not official list prices.
