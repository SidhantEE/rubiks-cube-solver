Enclosure options

Ten ways to hold six NEMA 17 motors around the 56 mm cube, ranked by how much plastic they need. In every one, each side motor bolts by its face and also rests on something under its body that runs down to the base. Every design keeps the motor face 32 mm from the cube face, so the adapter and firmware do not change.

Which one

J, Rail sleds, is my pick if you are willing to buy a few parts. It needs very little printing, the steel brackets are stiffer than anything printed, and it is the only bolted design where loading is one screw per side motor.

B, Skeleton cross, is the pick if you want to print everything and buy nothing. It is the current, checked design with the excess width removed, so it needs the least new design work.

E, Extrusion cube, is the stiffest for its plastic and the closest to what the fast robots use. It costs the most in bought parts and needs twelve square cuts.

Every design has the same loading problem: each adapter tip sits 4 mm inside the cube, so motors have to back away before a cube can go in. The designs differ mainly in how many motors must move and how.

Where the ideas came from

  • The Rubik's Contraption (Ben Katz and Jared Di Carlo, 0.38 s). Their box is laser-cut acrylic on scavenged 80/20 extrusion, each shaft can be loosened and slid out to load the cube, and two cameras look at opposite corners. Behind E, H and the sliding motors in I and J.
  • Coobycs. Six NEMA 17 motors on V-slot profiles joined by 3D-printed connectors that also hold the motors. Behind E.
  • Tesseract. Its write-up stresses a frame that does not flex under stepper torque, because flex jams the cube. I could only read the search summary of this page, not the build steps.
  • Instructables Rubik's Cube Solver (Teensy 4.1, TMC2208). Uses mounts that the motors slide into. Behind the body-gripping sockets in I. Search summary only.
  • Flatland and Rose robot. A 3D-printed frame with six steppers and four webcams, the same family as A and B.
  • Cubie. Their jams came from loose shaft adapters, which needed many iterations. A reminder that the adapter fit matters as much as the frame.
