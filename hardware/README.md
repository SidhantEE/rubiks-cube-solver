# Hardware

Nothing here has been built or tested yet. Dimensions come from datasheets and
the wiring follows the firmware in `firmware/cube_solver/cube_solver.ino`.

## Frame

The frame is generated by `cad/frame.py`. Print the STL files in `cad/stl`:

| Part | Qty | How it prints |
|---|---|---|
| `frame_base.stl` | 1 | As it sits, floor on the bed |
| `frame_pedestal.stl` | 1 | Upside down, top plate on the bed |
| `frame_tower.stl` | 2 | Standing on its foot |
| `frame_tower_tall.stl` | 2 | Standing on its foot |
| `frame_top.stl` | 1 | As it sits, flat face on the bed, collar up |
| `drive_adapter_...stl` | 6 | Standing on its wide end, tip up, 100% infill |

PLA, 0.2 mm layers, no supports. `docs/parts-viewer.html` shows the assembly in 3D.

How it goes together:

- The layout follows the Flatland and Rose record robot of 2016
  (github.com/jayflatland/HighFrequencyTwister): box columns on a flat base,
  with a bridge for the sixth motor.
- The base sits flat on the bench. The bottom motor hangs inside the pedestal
  from four M3 screws, shaft pointing up. The pedestal screws to the base from
  underneath with four M3 × 10 screws; do this before the towers go on.
- Each side motor drops into the cradle on top of its tower from above, with
  the shaft (and the adapter, if fitted) in the slot of the front wall. The
  body sits on the shelf between four walls. Four M3 × 10 screws go through
  the front wall into the motor face. Then two M3 × 8 clamp screws go through
  the holes in the side walls and pinch the motor body near its back end.
  Snug only: they cut their own thread in the plastic. Fit the motor to the
  tower before the tower goes on the base, because the face screws go in from
  the cube side. Mount it with the cable connector facing up.
- Each tower bolts to the base with two M3 × 10 screws and nuts. The slots let
  it slide 5 mm in or out. Do not use longer screws here: they would stick out
  under the base. The two tall towers go opposite each other.
- The top motor stands inside the collar on the bridge, shaft pointing down,
  held by four M3 × 10 screws from below and two M3 × 8 clamp screws through
  the collar. The bridge sits on the posts of the two tall towers and is held
  by four M3 × 16 screws, two per tower.
- Every motor face is 32 mm from its cube face, so all six adapters are the same.

Loading the cube: undo the four bridge screws and lift the bridge off with its
motor. Loosen the four towers and slide them back; the adapter tips sit 4 mm
inside the cube, so the cube cannot lift out until they are clear. Set the
cube on the bottom adapter, push the towers in and refit the bridge.

## Shaft adapter

The adapter slides over the motor's 5 mm shaft, is locked onto the shaft's flat
with an M3 × 6 screw, and ends in a square tip that sits in the recess under
the cube's centre cap.

The tip size in the model is a placeholder. Before printing six of them, pop
one centre cap off the cube and measure:

1. the width of the square recess,
2. its depth,
3. how far the tension screw's head stands up inside it.

Put those into `TIP_SQ`, `TIP_DEPTH` and `TIP_SCREW_D` in `cad/bench_stand.py`
and regenerate.

Without calipers, print `cad/stl/drive_adapter_set.stl` instead (standing on
the wide end, tips up, 100% infill, 0.2 mm layers, no supports). It is the
adapter nine times, with tip widths from 13.5 to 15.5 mm in 0.25 mm steps and
the width engraved on the side of each. Use the one whose tip goes into the
recess without force and does not rock, then put its width into `TIP_SQ` and
regenerate to print the other five. Only the width varies: the tip depth and
screw clearance in the set are still the placeholder values.

## Cube

YJ YuLong V2 M, 56 mm, magnetic.

- Tension it tighter than feels right for hand solving. Builders of fast
  robots found that a loose cube deforms and comes apart at speed.
- Lubricate it with silicone lube.
- The magnets pull each layer into alignment at every quarter turn. That
  should help the robot, because a layer left a few degrees off jams the next
  turn. This has not been tested on this build.
- Expect to break a cube while tuning. Keep a spare.

## Wiring

Logic is 3.3 V everywhere. The Teensy 4.1 is not 5 V tolerant: never connect
5 V to any of its pins or to a driver's logic supply.

```
24 V supply ──┬── VM  on every driver  (22 AWG or heavier, not jumper wires)
              └── GND on every driver
                  100 µF 50 V capacitor across VM and GND at each driver

Teensy 4.1 (powered over USB from the computer)
  3.3V ──────── VIO on every driver (logic supply)
  GND  ──────── GND on every driver (shared with the 24 V supply's ground)
  11 (MOSI) ─── SDI on every driver
  12 (MISO) ─── SDO on every driver
  13 (SCK)  ─── SCK on every driver
  23 ────────── EN  on every driver (LOW = on)
```

Each driver also gets its own STEP, DIR and chip-select (CS, sometimes
labelled CSN) wire:

| Face | STEP | DIR | CS |
|---|---|---|---|
| U (top) | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| R (right) | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| F (front) | 8 | 9 | 10 |
| D (bottom) | 14 | 15 | 16 |
| L (left) | 17 | 18 | 19 |
| B (back) | 20 | 21 | 22 |

On every driver, tie the CLK pin to GND so the chip uses its internal clock.
Follow the pin labels printed on the board; this document names signals, not
pin positions.

Jumper wires are fine for the signal lines above. Keep the SPI wires short,
about 10 to 20 cm. The 24 V supply and the motor coils carry 2 A and need
22 AWG wire or heavier, and must not pass through a breadboard.

### Motor cable

The motor's cable ends in a 4-pin plug wired red, blue, green, black. The
coils are:

| Coil | Wires |
|---|---|
| A | black (A+) and blue (A−) |
| B | green (B+) and red (B−) |

The driver's four motor pins are arranged so that each coil's two wires sit
next to each other. The plug as supplied alternates them (B, A, B, A), so a
motor plugged straight in buzzes instead of turning. Swap the blue and green
pins in the plug, giving red, green, blue, black.

Never plug or unplug a motor while its driver has 24 V. That can destroy the
driver.

## Motor current

There is no potentiometer to set. The firmware sets the current over SPI at
start-up: 2.0 A RMS while moving, about 30% of that while holding still. The
`CURRENT <mA>` command changes it.

At 2.0 A the driver is at the limit of what it can do without a fan. If a
driver overheats it reduces the motor current, which shows up as missed turns
during long runs. `STATUS` reports an overtemperature warning.

## Power-up order

1. Plug the Teensy into USB.
2. Switch on the 24 V supply.
3. Send `STATUS`. Each face should report `version=0x30 ... ok`.

If the 24 V supply comes on after the Teensy, the drivers start unconfigured.
The firmware sets them up again before the next move, so no reset is needed.

## Bench test

Do this with one motor on `bench_stand.stl` before building the frame.

1. Wire one driver as the U face and connect one motor.
2. Send `STATUS` and confirm U reports `ok`.
3. Send `U`. The face should make one quarter turn in 60 ms.
4. Send `TURN 55`, then `U` several times, then `TURN 50`, and so on.
5. Stop at the first setting where the face ends up short of a full quarter
   turn. That is the motor losing steps.

Holding 40 to 45 ms reliably means the design performs as calculated. If the
motor turns the wrong way, flip its entry in `INVERT_DIR` in the firmware.
